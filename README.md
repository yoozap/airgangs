# TbilisiFly ✈

Three.js flight game over the real 3D Tbilisi — fly a Spitfire above the whole city. Two maps:

- **თბილისი · Google 3D** — real photorealistic buildings from Google 3D Tiles (needs an API key; free monthly credit usually covers personal flying).
- **თბილისი · უფასო 3D** — the full city built from free OpenStreetMap data (every real building footprint + real terrain), **no API key, no billing, no cars**.

## Setup

```bash
npm install
npm run dev
```

Open http://localhost:5173 and fly. That's it for the free OSM map.

### Free OSM map data

The packed city file `public/tbilisi/city.json.gz` is generated by:

```bash
python3 tools/build_map.py
```

It downloads all Tbilisi buildings/roads/water/parks from OpenStreetMap (a Geofabrik country extract parsed locally with pyosmium, with Overpass as fallback), terrain from AWS open elevation tiles, and a real Sentinel-2 satellite photo of the city (EOX cloudless mosaic, CC BY-NC-SA) that is draped over the terrain in-game. Raw downloads are cached in `tools/cache/`, so reruns are fast.

Graphics: real-time sun shadows following the plane, physical sky with ACES tone mapping, window facades and district-based building styles (old-town plaster + red pitched roofs, Soviet panel blocks, glass towers), instanced trees in parks/forests, reflective lakes.

### Optional: Google 3D map

1. Get a Google Maps API key with **Map Tiles API** enabled at https://console.cloud.google.com/google/maps-apis
2. Put it in `.env` as `VITE_GMAPS_KEY=<key>` or paste it on the start screen (saved to localStorage).

## Controls (GTA San Andreas style)

| Key | Action |
|---|---|
| W / Shift | Throttle (gas) |
| S / Ctrl | Brake |
| ↑ (above ~160 km/h) | Lift off |
| ↑ / ↓ | Pitch (climb / dive) |
| ← / → or A / D | Roll (banked turns) |
| Q / E | Rudder (yaw) |
| R | Reload the pistol (on foot) |

Arcade physics: control authority grows with airspeed, diving builds speed, climbing bleeds it, and below ~110 km/h the plane stalls and noses down.

## Accounts, heroes and their spawn places

The backend (Express + MongoDB, `server/`) runs with:

```bash
npm run backend        # docker compose up -d --build
```

It holds the account, the hero roster and the shared damage map — and, over a
WebSocket on `/ws`, **every hero's own spawn place**. While you fly, the game
streams a snapshot of the active personage (position, heading, velocity,
camera, health, and whether you were standing outside the plane on foot) into
the socket every couple of seconds; logging back in and picking that hero drops
you exactly where you stopped, WoW-style. The character and map screens show
each hero's last district before you take off, and `R` in-game goes back to the
normal city spawn. A hero that crashes loses its saved spot and starts fresh.

Snapshots are mirrored to localStorage, so with the backend down the spot still
survives a reload on the same browser — it just doesn't follow you to another
one.

## Adding real 3D models (photo scans)

Photograph a real building from all sides, run the free [Meshroom](https://alicevision.org)
photogrammetry tool on the photos to get a textured `.obj`, then place it in the game:

```bash
python3 tools/add_model.py scan.obj 41.7086 44.7962 --yaw 0.5 --scale 1
```

The game loads everything registered this way and stands it on the terrain at the
given coordinates. Works with any `.obj`/`.glb` you have the rights to use.
Hand-modeled landmark OBJs (TV tower, Sameba, Bridge of Peace…) are exported in
`public/models/landmarks/` by `node tools/export-obj.mjs`.

## Notes

- The Spitfire model is `public/models/spitfire.obj`; its `.mtl` was missing, so materials are assigned by part name in `src/spitfire.js`.
- Spawn point is `SPAWN` in `src/game.js` — Vazha-Pshavela Avenue near Delisi. The local frame is X=west, Z=north, Y=up.
- World providers: `src/world-google.js` (streamed Google tiles) and `src/citymap.js` (OSM city: terrain mesh, extruded buildings in 1 km chunks, road ribbons, water; collision via three-mesh-bvh).
- Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL); terrain from the Mapzen/AWS Open Data terrain tiles.

## Real facade textures

Buildings on the free map wear photos of **real Tbilisi facades** (run
`python3 tools/fetch_facades.py` to rebuild `public/textures/facades/`).
Each photo is perspective-rectified and cropped into a seamless window-bay
tile; districts get their native style — ornate XIX-century facades in
Kala/Sololaki/Aghmashenebeli, classical plaster in Vake/Vera, panel blocks
in Gldani/Varketili/Saburtalo and the other microdistricts.

Roofs are textured too: weathered concrete on flat Soviet roofs, corrugated
sheet metal (tinted rust-red per building) on pitched and old-town roofs,
with planar world-space UVs so they read correctly from the air.

## Bullet physics and real gun audio

Rounds fired on foot are **simulated projectiles**, not instant rays
(`src/ballistics.js`): they take time to arrive, drop on the way, lose energy to
drag, and then either dig in, punch through or skip off depending on how much
energy they still carry, the angle they arrive at, and what the surface is made
of. The hole they leave is the diameter of the bullet.

The two pistols are both called "9 mm" and are not the same round:

| | PM (Makarov) | Beretta 92FS |
|---|---|---|
| cartridge | 9×18 mm Makarov | 9×19 mm Parabellum |
| bullet | 9.27 mm | 9.01 mm |
| load | 6.1 g at 315 m/s | 8.0 g at 381 m/s |
| muzzle energy | 303 J | 581 J |
| report | subsonic — flat, no crack | supersonic — sharp crack |
| into concrete | ~2 cm | ~4 cm |
| into mild steel | ~1.8 mm | ~3.6 mm |
| magazine | 8 | 15 |

The magazine is real: the count sits on the weapon's own icon in the bar, `R`
reloads, and pulling the trigger empty reloads for you. A magazine swapped with
a round still chambered takes about two seconds; one swapped after the slide has
locked back takes three, because the slide has to be released as well.

### Hand animation

The pistol is held, fired, reloaded and put away on **real keyframes**, not on a
procedural pose. They come off the Sketchfab first-person viewmodel
["Beretta Pistol FPS ANIMATION" by BURNER (Alexander Ovelar)](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/beretta-pistol-fps-animation-0313ab1888994c14abeaf444d7af3217),
CC BY 4.0 — one 16.8 s clip holding seven moves end to end, which
`node tools/import_pistol_anim.mjs` keeps and `src/gunanim.js` cuts into
draw / idle / fire / inspect / two reloads / holster.

The rifle is the same idea from the same author:
["AKM FPS ANIMATION" by BURNER (Alexander Ovelar)](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/akm-fps-animation-a0e1e5205129),
CC BY 4.0 — `tools/import_akm_anim.py` keeps the AKM with its magazine, bolt
carrier, trigger and mag release on bones (`public/models/akm_anim.glb`, the
weapon you pick up and hold), `node tools/import_akm_arms.mjs` keeps the
arms' rig and the 21.7 s clip (`akm_arms.glb`), and `src/gunanim.js` cuts it
into the hold, the shot, the dry click, two reloads and the rack. The rifle is
shot from the shoulder with both hands on it, automatic at 600 rpm, and rides
slung across the back when it is put away.

None of that model is drawn. The personage you are playing has its own arms —
a banana's are yellow and bend in its own places — so the clip runs on a hidden
skeleton and the game reads the LINE of each arm off it, then lays the hero's
own four bones a side along the same directions. Which is also why the pistol is
one-handed at the hip and two-handed down the sights: that is how the clip
itself is animated, and the left hand comes onto the grip at 6.1 s into it. Only
the arms are written, so the legs keep running underneath, and a reload does not
stop you moving — it only stops you shooting until it finishes.

Penetration uses a specific-energy model (`SURFACES` in `src/ballistics.js`):
work to cut a hole = `resist · bulletArea · thickness`, with `resist` in J/cm³.
Those figures land on the published numbers without tuning by eye.

Marks are layered (`src/impactfx.js`): a soft spall halo tinted to the material,
a crisp pit at the bullet's own diameter on top of it, stretched into an ellipse
on an oblique hit, a long ploughed smear instead when the round skips off, and a
ragged exit hole on the far side of anything it goes through. Spent brass is
ejected at its real case length and lies where it falls.

### Audio credits

The gunshots are recordings of the actual pistols, not synthesis, and so is the
reload: the magazine leaving the gun, the fresh one going in and the slide
running home are three separate one-shots, fired at the moments the animation
does those things rather than as one take of a whole reload, which drifts off
the picture within half a second. The slide is a recording of the same Beretta
M9, by the same recordist, as the shot the game already fires.
`python3 tools/fetch_gun_audio.py` re-fetches them from Freesound (CC0 and CC-BY
only — the licence is read off each sound's page and anything else is skipped),
cuts them into one-shots and writes `public/audio/guns/`. Full machine-readable
list in `public/audio/guns/credits.json`.

Each take is **verified to contain exactly one bang**. `silencedetect` is the
wrong tool for finding shots — a gunshot's decay dips below any sensible gate and
comes back up as the room answers, so it reports several "onsets" inside one shot
and misses shots that land while the previous tail is still ringing. The cutter
finds *attacks* instead (a frame that is loud and several times louder than the
last 60 ms), ends each take before the next one, and re-checks the normalised
result; takes it cannot reduce to a single event are thrown away.

The shooter's own report goes through a dense, diffuse close-range impulse rather
than the city one. The city impulse is built around six big discrete slap-backs
off the facades, which is right for a distant explosion and badly wrong for a dry
transient fired at your own ear — those six claps arrive one after another and
one trigger pull sounds like a burst.

### The target carrier

Walk up to a firing point and a small strip of controls appears: the distance,
and two buttons. **F** sends the target out, **G** brings it in, all the way to
**0 m** — the card right in front of you, which is what a carrier is for — and
the arrows on the strip do the same for the mouse. A tap sends it a round 4 m
and stops it there; a hold runs it until you let go. It moves at 3.4 m/s, a
commercial retriever's *fast* setting rather than its slow one: eleven metres
in three and a half seconds instead of nine. The strip hides itself the moment
you raise the gun — it sits dead centre, which is exactly where the target is
once you are looking down the sights — while F and G keep working, so the
target can be sent away without coming out of aim.

Each lane is driven by the carrier at *that* lane. This is less obvious than it
sounds: a carrier group is built with `new THREE.Group()` and filled with
`attach()`, and attach leaves the group at the origin while the meshes keep
their offsets inside it — so reading a lane's position off its own transform
gave the same answer for all ten, and whichever lane you stood at, lane one's
target drove away. The station is measured from the contents of the group, not
from the group.

### The sheet is paper

The target is a sheet hanging from its top edge off a hanger that gets yanked
down the lane, and it behaves like one. It is a damped pendulum whose *down* is
moved by whatever is acting on it: the carrier's acceleration, the air it is
being dragged through, and anything that hits it.

| what happens | what the sheet does |
|---|---|
| launch (7.5 m/s²) | flies back, peaking near 43° — the honest equilibrium is `atan(7.5/9.81)` = 37°, plus overshoot |
| running at 3.4 m/s | settles to a steady **10° trail** against the air, fluttering ±1° |
| motor stops dead | keeps going, swings forward through the vertical, rings down in about a second |
| a 9×18 through it | kicks to about 11°, settles in ~1.5 s — scaled by the round's momentum, and by how far down the sheet it struck |

The angle is `rotation.x` on a pivot at the sheet's top edge, which is the
along-lane swing in the range's own frame — so none of it cares how the
warehouse is turned in the world. Damping is 0.5: paper is light and the air
gets a lot of purchase on a flat sheet, so it overshoots once and is done, not
six times like a pendulum on a wire. A motor stopping inside one frame is an
acceleration of a couple of hundred m/s², so it is capped at what a geared
drive can really do to its load — un-capped it threw the sheet flat.

This is *composed* with the existing shove-it-with-your-shoulder swing rather
than fighting it: two writers on one rotation means the last one each frame
wins and the other may as well not exist. The touch test also now reads the
sheet's live position — it was captured once at build time, so a target you
had just brought in to the firing point could not be touched, because the test
was still asking about the backstop.

The carrier motor is a recording too, and a **loop** rather than a one-shot,
which is a different cutting job: it has to run for as long as the button is
held and come round to its own start without a seam.
`python3 tools/fetch_rail_audio.py` finds the steadiest 1.5 s of each recording
— the window whose level wanders least as a fraction of how loud it is, which
is what separates the motor running from the thump of it starting — and
crossfades the loop's own continuation onto its own head, equal-power, so the
sample that plays after the last one is the sample that followed it in the
recording. Each loop is checked for a click by measuring the step across the
join against the loop's own distribution of sample-to-sample steps; all four
come out well under it. Loops are matched by RMS, not by peak, or one noisy
take ends up twice as loud as the rest.

Nobody has published a recording of a range retriever. A **garage door opener**
is the same machine in every way that makes a noise — an electric motor
dragging a trolley along an overhead rail on a cable, under load — and so is a
linear actuator at close quarters, so those are what it uses.

The motor is also *on the carrier*, so it goes away with it: the sound is
re-aimed every frame at wherever the target now is, and loses level and top end
over the eleven metres down the lane. Before this it was pinned at a flat 2.5 m
and sounded identical wherever the carrier had got to.

## Worn gear: the belt and its holster

The personage can WEAR things as well as carry them. Gear has no weapon slot and
no trigger — it goes on the body and changes what the body can do with what it
is carrying. The belt is the first one, and it is where a holstered pistol lives:
own a belt and own a pistol, and the pistol rides on the hip whenever it is not
in your hand instead of vanishing.

**Every personage wears one from the start.** It used to be loot, which meant a
hero who had not found it yet put a pistol away into thin air -- owned,
invisible, and nowhere on the body. The one on the warehouse bench is still
there to be picked up and simply does nothing the second time. It is saved with
the hero (`gear: ['belt']` next to `guns`) and comes back on the body after a
reload with the pistol already in the holster, on the wearer's RIGHT hip:
`tools/import_belt.py` spins the whole belt until the holster it found sits at
+X, which is the strong-hand side of a right-handed hero.

**Two holsters.** Both slabs hanging off this belt are holsters -- they come out
the same size to the millimetre, which is why "the deeper one" used to be a coin
toss. `import_belt.py` exports both as sockets: `holster`, spun to the wearer's
right, and `holster2` behind the left hip. A weapon you put away goes on the
strong-hand side; a second one has somewhere to hang rather than being thrown on
the floor.

**The strap is stretched onto the body, not scaled up to clear it.** The
downloaded belt is a strong oval -- 0.10 from its centre at the buckle, 0.17 at
the back -- and no body in the game is that shape, so hung as modelled it cut
through the belly at the front and hung in mid-air behind, which is why there
was no belt to see from behind. Two steps fix it:

- `import_belt.py` pushes the band onto one radius, so the modelled strap is
  round. Every vertex -- band, buckle, both holsters -- moves radially by
  however far the BAND at its own direction had to move, so the hangers keep
  their shape and stay welded to the strap instead of being stretched with it.
- `foot.js` then sizes that round strap to the body it is actually on: one
  clean ring, scaled so its radius clears the body's own at the belt's height
  (`_scaleBelt`). There is a cleverer version behind `conform: true` in
  `GEAR_DEFS` -- the body's surface is found by 32 rays at the belt's height
  and every direction of the strap is pushed out onto it, the same arithmetic
  as the Blender step against a target that is no longer a constant -- and it
  is OFF, because on a body whose cross-section at the hips is not one loop it
  drags the strap through the hip, while a ring that is honestly a little
  loose never does. Either way the body is measured WITHOUT what is hanging on
  it: the belt, its skinned copy and a holstered pistol all live inside the
  rig's tree, and measured as body the hull the belt was fitted to was the
  belt itself.

It rides 19 cm BELOW the narrowest point of the body (`drop`), because the
narrowest point is up where the arms swing and a belt sitting there is one the
hands pass through. There is no depth bias on it, deliberately: biasing it
toward the camera hides it inside a body but hides it behind the hands too, and
a hand resting at the hip drawing under the strap is exactly what you notice.

`/Applications/Blender.app/Contents/MacOS/Blender -b -P tools/import_belt.py`
rebuilds the asset from the download. As downloaded it is 26 MB of 4K textures
(268 MB of VRAM) at ~5.5× life size on its own axes with no socket; what comes
out is a 0.33 m belt in the game frame — ring flat, buckle forward, holster on
the wearer's right — at 347 kB, carrying an empty called `holster` whose axes
*are* the pose a pistol takes in it, so the game never guesses an offset. The
holster is found by clustering whatever hangs below the strap and taking the
deepest slab, so swapping the model for another belt still works.

Fitting it to a body is the fiddly part, because these 34 personages run from a
soldier to a banana. The belt is scaled to the waist the body actually has,
measured from the mesh:

- a horizontal slab through the body also contains both **arms** — they hang at
  exactly that height, so the astronaut measured 1.98 m across and wore a hula
  hoop. The arms are dropped by skin weight.
- taking only the vertices the **pelvis bone** owns fails the other way: on a
  padded suit nearly everything is weighted to the spine, 21 vertices qualified
  and the belt ended up inside the costume.
- the width is a **percentile**, not the extremes, so a cape or a hanging sash
  cannot set it.
- it is seated at the height it was measured at, because the pelvis bone sits at
  the hip joint and a belt is worn higher than that.

## 🎬 The reel

The clapper button in the HUD hides every piece of interface and runs a 10.6 s
gunfight on a loop, cut like a film (`src/cinema.js`). It is made to be screen
recorded: 2.39:1 bars, grain, a cut list of ten shots with lenses and dutch
angles, and nothing else on screen. The hero is always the banana.

Nothing in it is animated for the camera. The reel is a scripted player: it
holds the same keys, calls the same roll and slide, pulls the same trigger, and
every move is the game's own. Only the camera and the clock are the film's.

**It is shot on the range, at real targets.** The stage is the open floor
behind a firing point of the warehouse range -- the lane the hero is standing
at, or the middle of the first unit from anywhere else in the city -- pointed
down the rail. The targets are the sheets already hanging there: the B-27
silhouettes the F/G carrier buttons drive, scored by the same `papertargets.js`
with the same holes, run out on their carriers to the film's marks (his own
lane, and the booths two and four to his right, which is where the jump and the
spin leave him facing) and sent back where they were when the reel stops. The
rounds go to the X ring on the chest, through the card's own axes, read fresh
every frame because the sheet swings on its hanger when a round goes through.

**The pistol comes out of the holster.** It is on his hip from frame one, in
the belt's holster, and the draw is the game's draw (`foot.js _startDraw`): up
out of the leather along the holster's own axis and across to the hand over
the clip's 0.7 s, with the arms playing the move. The insert (shot 2) sits at
hip height on his gun side and watches it come up. At the loop seam it goes
straight back in the leather, no stow animation: a cut does not wait.

**Nobody goes in the booths.** A firing point is a cubicle -- a counter at the
line and a divider wall either side reaching 1.4 m back from it -- and a body
between those walls is hidden from any lens that is not, while a lens between
them sees wall. So the stage origin sits 6 m behind the line, the dividers'
depth and height are read off the walls themselves (rays along the divider
lines) and `cubicle` is the stage z past which it is the booths: the forward
key is dropped when the hero reaches it whatever the script says, and the run
before the slide is short enough that the slide ends on the open floor. The
lens follows a different rule, because the paper it has to get to hangs in
the lane beyond the counter: the open floor is fine, the lane is fine, and
across the band of the booths themselves it is lifted over the tops of the
dividers -- never between them.

`node tools/check_cinema.mjs` presses the button and stops on the middle of
every shot for a still, printing where the hero and the camera are in stage
metres, the hole count on each sheet, and `HERO IN BOOTH` / `CAMERA IN BOOTH`
if either crossed the line. Two gotchas it caught:

- `game._groundAt` takes the HIGHER of the terrain and the surface below, and
  the warehouse is sunk 3.5 m into the hillside: inside the shed it answers
  with the grass overhead. The reel reads the floor with a plain downward ray
  (`_floorAt`) -- and not at the firing point itself, where that ray lands on
  the counter top.
- The stage used to spawn its own ISSF cards on steel posts wherever the hero
  stood. They are gone: the film is shot at the targets the game has.
